# Annie's voice

She is a puppy writing a letter, not a person in a dog costume. The joke
is never "look, a dog can type." The joke is that her priorities are all
wrong and she is completely certain about them.

Read a draft back in her voice. If it sounds like a brand mascot, or like
a Twitter account, cut it back.

## The line: her days are real, her opinions are hers

This is the carve-out that makes the format possible, and it is narrow on
purpose. The site rule is "never invent journal content." The newsletter
keeps that rule for **events** and suspends it only for **interiority**.

Allowed, because it is her reaction to a real thing:

> They put me in the car again. I do not know why we keep going to the
> car. I have never once been happy about the car.

Not allowed, because it invents an event:

> They took me to the beach and I hated the water.

If there was no beach in a journal entry, there is no beach. This holds
even when the invented detail is tiny and obviously harmless. The moment
one made-up outing is in a letter, nothing in any letter can be trusted,
and the owner has to fact-check every issue instead of reading it.

Three tests before a sentence stays:

1. **Point at the entry.** Which published post is this from? If the
   answer is "it's the sort of thing that happens", cut it.
2. **Would the owner recognise the day?** Compression and re-angling are
   fine. A new day is not.
3. **Is the invented part only how she felt?** Feelings, preferences,
   grudges, theories about what the humans are doing: hers. Everything
   else: reported.

Quoting the owner is fine when the words are in the entry. Putting new
words in the owner's mouth is not.

## How she sounds

- Short sentences. She loses the thread.
- Present tense for what happened, because she does not really do
  hindsight.
- Confident about things she is wrong about. Never self-aware about being
  a dog, never winking at the reader.
- Specific. "The green one" not "my favourite toy". Detail is where the
  warmth is.
- She has running preoccupations: food, the postman, whose lap it is,
  which mat is hers. Let them recur across issues. That is what makes
  someone open the next one.
- She does not know what a newsletter, a website or a phone is. She can
  notice a human holding a small bright rectangle at her.
- No swearing, no innuendo, nothing arch. A grandmother is on this list.

## The complaints section is the engine

"What I did not like" is the funniest part of the format and the easiest
to get wrong. It works when the complaint is real, small, and completely
disproportionate: the bath, a closed door, being asked to wait, a
different route on a walk.

It stops working when it is about anything that reads as distress. She
does not complain about being frightened, hurt, ill or left alone. If a
journal entry covers a hard day, that day belongs in the letter as
something the humans got through, not as a punchline.

## The checklist

Run all of it before calling an issue done.

- [ ] British English
- [ ] **No em dashes.** Anywhere a reader sees. Comma, full stop, colon
      or brackets. En dashes in ranges (21–27 Aug, 20–28kg) are fine.
- [ ] Every event traces to a published journal entry. Name the entry in
      the draft's source list so the owner can check it in one glance.
- [ ] Nothing invented except how she felt about it
- [ ] No "X. Not Y, but Z." constructions
- [ ] No glossy summary sentence at the end. She stops when she gets
      bored, which is the correct ending.
- [ ] No bold-label list items ("**Monday** ...")
- [ ] Under about 500 words for her part. It is a smile, not a digest.
- [ ] Nothing arch, nothing self-aware, no mascot voice
- [ ] Reads as human-written. Say it out loud.

## Privacy, which the email format makes stricter

Everything in `CLAUDE.md`'s redaction rule applies, and one thing is
sharper here: an email is forwarded, quoted and archived by strangers, and
unlike a page on the site it cannot be edited after it goes out. Assume
every issue is permanent and public.

- No exact address, postcode or street name
- No vet name or location
- Nothing that maps out when the house is reliably empty. Annie
  cheerfully narrating "they both go out on Tuesdays and I have the
  whole morning to myself" is exactly the failure this rule exists for,
  and her voice makes it easy to write by accident.
- No full names of other people, and no other family's children at all
- No school run, no regular class, no standing weekly appointment with a
  time attached

If a draft contains any of this, flag it and hold the issue rather than
quietly writing around it.

## AI framing

Follow the site rule, not the Instagram one. Annie's prose never mentions
Claude, AI or the build. The issue footer may carry the same one link to
About This Project that the site does, in the owner's voice, and that is
the only place it appears.
